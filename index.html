01 · The grid

A universe made of squares

Picture an endless sheet of graph paper. Every square — a cell — is in exactly one of two states: alive or dead. That's the whole universe.

Ours is a small window onto it. Tap or click a cell to flip it. Keyboard: arrow keys to move, Enter to flip. Nothing moves yet — time hasn't started.

Alive: 0 / 100

02 · Neighbors

Every cell has eight neighbors

A cell's neighbors are the eight squares touching it — up, down, left, right, and the four diagonals. Everything that happens in the game depends only on how many of those eight are alive.

Hover (or tap) a cell to light up its neighborhood. Tap also flips the cell, so you can watch the count change. Cells on the border of this little window simply have fewer neighbors.

03 · The four rules

Birth, survival, and death

Time moves in ticks called generations. On every tick, all cells update at once, using just four rules. Each card below shows one rule in action — flip between Before and After, or press Play.

05 · Pattern zoo

A field guide to the residents

Out of the four rules, a menagerie appears. Still lifes never change. Oscillators repeat after a fixed period. Spaceships crawl across the grid. Press Try in sandbox on any card to drop it into the sandbox.

06 · Emergence

Simple rules, complex worlds

Nothing in the rules mentions gliders, guns, or oscillators — yet they appear anyway. Complex global behavior emerges from nothing but local, identical, mindless rules.

Other rules (advanced)

The game's four rules are written B3/S23: a cell is Born with 3 neighbors and Survives with 2 or 3. Changing the numbers creates a different universe. Everything else on this page, including every pattern in the zoo and the Gosper glider gun, is designed for Conway's rule and will misbehave under the others. Stamping a pattern switches back to the rule it needs.

08 · Going further

Down the rabbit hole

John Conway & the 1970 column

The British mathematician John Horton Conway (1937–2020) invented the game in 1970. It reached the world that October in Martin Gardner's "Mathematical Games" column in Scientific American, and promptly consumed a generation of computer time.

Life can compute

The game is Turing complete: gliders can carry signals, guns can clock them, and collisions act as logic gates. Builders have assembled working computers — even a Turing machine, by Paul Rendell in 2000 — entirely out of Life patterns.
